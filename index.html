      What you are measuring

      Distribution
      Where the organisms are. The ground under a tree often carries little grass — that is a statement about distribution.
      Abundance
      How many there are. For organisms you can pick out individually, abundance is a count. For a carpet of grass, moss or seaweed, where individuals cannot be told apart, abundance is a percentage cover.

      The quadrat

      A square frame of known area laid on the ground; you record only what lies inside it. The frames in this lab are strung into a 5 × 5 grid, so each small square is 4% of the quadrat — count the small squares a species more than half fills, multiply by four, and you have its percentage cover.

      The boundary rule used here: an individual counts if its centre lies inside the frame. Agree a rule before you start and stick to it, or two people surveying the same quadrat will get different answers.

      Random sampling — and why it has to be random

      1. Lay two tape measures at right angles along the edges of the area, making a pair of axes.
      2. Use a random number generator to get a pair of coordinates.
      3. Put the quadrat at those coordinates and record what is inside.
      4. Repeat. Ten quadrats is the usual minimum; a clumped species needs more.

      The generator, not you, picks the spot. The moment a person chooses — drawn to the interesting patch, or to the easy walk — the sample stops representing the area and the estimate is biased. The My own spots method and the Investigate tab let you measure that bias for yourself.

      Estimating the whole population

      A quadrat is a known fraction of the area, so scaling up is arithmetic:

      mean number per quadrat × (total area ÷ area of one quadrat) = estimated population

      With a 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrat (0.25 m²) on a 20 m × 20 m field (400 m²), one quadrat is 1/1600 of the field. A mean of 2.4 daisies per quadrat gives 2.4 × 1600 = 3,840 daisies. For percentage cover there is nothing to scale: the mean percentage cover of your quadrats is the estimate for the whole field.

      Transects — sampling a gradient

      When abundance changes across an area rather than varying at random, random sampling hides the pattern in an average. Instead, run a line — a transect — along the gradient and place quadrats at regular intervals: across a footpath, up a shore, out from the trunk of a tree. This is systematic sampling, not random, and that is deliberate: you want the samples spread evenly along the gradient.

      • Line transect — record only the organisms touching the line itself.
      • Belt transect — record a continuous band along the line.
      • Interrupted belt transect — a quadrat at fixed intervals. This is what the lab builds, and what most fieldwork uses.

      Results go on a line graph of abundance against distance, or a kite diagram. On a flat habitat, such as the mown lawn in this lab, a transect shows a flat profile — which is itself the answer: there is no gradient, so use random quadrats.

      The ACFOR scale

      Shore surveys often record abundance on a quick descriptive scale instead of counting: Abundant, Common, Frequent, Occasional, Rare. It is fast and needs no counting, but it is subjective and you cannot do much arithmetic with it. This lab shows the ACFOR band beside the percentage cover on the rocky shore so you can see how they line up.

      Where this simulation is honest, and where it is not

      • Honest: the habitat has a real, fixed population that the sampling does not know about, so your sampling error is genuine. Random coordinates are genuinely random, clumped species are genuinely clumped, and the percentage cover you read off the grid really is what is drawn inside the frame.
      • Not honest: no weather, no trampling your own transect, no plants that are hard to tell apart, no time pressure, and the frame always lands flat. Real fieldwork is where those turn up — this is the rehearsal.
